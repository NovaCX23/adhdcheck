# ADHD game prototype: literature and unmet need (evidence base, collected 2026-10-19)

Scope: evidence collected for the team to judge. No product decision is made here. Every number is quoted from an abstract or a fetched primary page, with its population. "not found" means the search did not return a verifiable value, and no estimate is given.

Method note: PubMed records were retrieved with the PubMed tools and each PMID was checked against the returned title (all titles matched the entry below). Abstracts are the only text read; no full text was read. Source: PubMed (NCBI). Non-PubMed sources are marked "web" and carry their access limits in the reference list.

## Key takeaways for a pitch (each line cited)

1. Pooled community prevalence of ADHD in children and adolescents was 5.29% (102 studies, 171,756 subjects, age 18 or under) [1], and 3.4% (95% CI 2.6-4.5) in a later pooled analysis of 41 studies in 27 countries [3]. Variability was explained mainly by methods, not geography [1][2].
2. In clinical settings, pooled ADHD/HD prevalence was 32.4% (95% CI 31-34%) in paediatric patients (311 studies, 653,558 paediatric participants), 8-9 times the community estimate [4].
3. England: the independent ADHD Taskforce (NHS England, Part 1, April 2025) states NHS waiting times are "up to 4+ years for children" and that fewer than one-third of children meeting strict diagnostic criteria have contact with any mental health service [web-A].
4. England administrative prevalence was reported as 2.55% in boys and 0.67% in girls, against an actual population prevalence in children of 5%, which the Taskforce calls under-recognition [web-A]. Girls with ADHD were less hyperactive than boys in a meta-analysis of studies from 1997 to 2017 [8], one plausible route to missed cases (this link is an inference to be tested, not a finding of [8]).
5. Children with ADHD cost more: overall weighted mean direct medical cost was $5,319 for children with ADHD versus $1,152 for counterparts (32 studies) [6]. Global cost-of-illness estimates ranged from $US831.38 to 20,538 per person (total costs, 44 studies, high-income countries only) [7].
6. CPTs alone are not diagnostic. Pooled CPT total/ADHD score: sensitivity 0.75 (0.66-0.82), specificity 0.71 (0.62-0.78); 19 studies, children and adolescents; authors say CPTs "should be used only within a more comprehensive diagnostic process" [9].
7. QbTest total score pooled sensitivity 0.78 (0.69-0.85) and specificity 0.70 (0.57-0.81) in 15 studies (2,058 participants); the authors state QbTest "should not be used as stand-alone screening or diagnostic tool" [10].
8. Early gamified and serious-game classifiers are promising but small and unreplicated: F-measure 78% (ADHD inattentive) and 75% (combined) on 52 patients [17]; Random Forest accuracy 81.818%, AUROC 83.333% on 51 children, all already diagnosed with ADHD, classifying presentations not ADHD versus non-ADHD [18].
9. The only randomised digital therapeutic in children found here, AKL-T01 (STARS-ADHD, n=348, age 8-12), moved the TOVA Attention Performance Index: mean change 0.93 (SD 3.15) versus 0.03 (SD 3.16) in control; median difference 0.88 (95% CI 0.24-1.49, p=0.0060); sponsored by Akili [26].
10. Non-drug add-ons have weak blinded evidence: neurofeedback with probably blinded ratings SMD 0.04 (95% CI -0.10 to 0.18; 38 RCTs) [34]; computerised cognitive training SMD 0.12 on ADHD total (probably blinded; 36 RCTs) [30]; confirmatory eTNS trial in 150 youths showed no significant effect (d = 0.09, P = 0.622) [28].

## B. Objective and digital assessment of ADHD in children

Reading guide: sensitivity (Se), specificity (Sp), AUC, accuracy. Reference standard is stated where the abstract gives it. Most studies compare children already diagnosed with controls, which inflates accuracy relative to real screening of an undifferentiated population.

## Gaps the game could address

Each gap below is an observation from the sources above, for the team to judge. None is a recommendation.

1. Access: waits up to "4+ years for children" in England [web-A] and fewer than one-third of strictly diagnosed children in contact with mental health services [web-A]. A tool that organises a parent's concerns and routes to clinical assessment addresses access, not diagnosis.
2. Parent-report weakness: parent-teacher agreement kappa 0.25 [24]; SDQ sensitivity 42.5%-45.0% in a referred sample [23]. Objective in-game data add an independent signal, but no study here shows that signal outperforms rating scales.
3. Accuracy ceiling: pooled CPT Se 0.75 and Sp 0.71 [9]; QbTest Se 0.78 and Sp 0.70 [10]. A game must not be expected to exceed these without evidence; guidelines and meta-analyses say such tests belong inside a full assessment [9][10][web-B].
4. Girls and inattentive profiles: administrative prevalence 0.67% in girls vs 2.55% in boys against 5% real prevalence [web-A]; boys more hyperactive than girls [8]. Sex-specific norms and inattention-focused tasks are untested gaps.
5. Ecological validity: VR classroom CPT outperformed TOVA in one 338-child sample [14]; distractor-embedded tasks showed pooled SMD 1.14 [22]. A game naturally embeds distractors.
6. Engagement and age: a serious game worked in children aged 6-11 (d=0.901) but not adolescents (d=0.191) [19]; AKL-T01 patients played 83% of expected sessions [26].
7. Follow-up missions (the subscription idea): the digital-treatment evidence is weak on blinded symptom outcomes [29][30][31][33][34] and negative for eTNS [28]; the AKL-T01 trial's primary outcome was a lab attention score [26]. Any "missions" claim should be limited to what is shown, and not to symptom improvement.
8. Evidence void: no study found of a game-based screen in an undifferentiated child population against a full clinical reference. The team's own validation study is the missing piece, and it needs ethics review and a clinical partner.
9. Risk the sources flag: using a result to gate waiting lists [10], over-reading a negative (NPV as low as 24% [12]), and ignoring comorbidity (anxiety 18.4%, ODD 34.7% [5]).
